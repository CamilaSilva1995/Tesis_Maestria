**ABSTRACT**

Metagenomic data analysis poses a major challenge for Data Science due
to the high volume, complexity, and compositional nature of the
information generated by high-throughput sequencing technologies. In
this context, the present study proposes a statistical and bioinformatic
analysis pipeline for characterizing the rhizosphere microbiome of
*Fragaria × ananassa* crops, aiming to identify patterns associated with
plant health status and to evaluate the potential of metagenomic data to
support analysis and decision-making processes in agriculture.

The study was conducted using metagenomic data provided by the company
Solena Ag. After a quality-control and preprocessing stage, 53 samples
(35 healthy and 18 unhealthy) were analyzed using reproducible tools
implemented in R and Bash. The methodological pipeline integrated data
conversion to the BIOM format, handling through Phyloseq, exploratory
analysis, calculation of alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon, and
Simpson), beta diversity metrics (Bray-Curtis, Jaccard, Manhattan,
Euclidean, and Jensen-Shannon), taxonomic exploration at different
hierarchical levels, and statistical hypothesis testing using Student\'s
t-test and the Mann--Whitney test.

The results show that healthy plants tend to exhibit greater microbial
diversity and a more balanced community, while unhealthy plants exhibit
a higher presence of microorganisms potentially associated with disease.
Nevertheless, the overall differences between both groups did not reach
statistical significance for the general diversity metrics, which
evidences the inherent complexity of agricultural microbiomes and the
need to incorporate analytical models with greater descriptive and
predictive capacity.

As a main contribution, this work integrates Data Science, statistical,
and bioinformatic tools into a reproducible pipeline for metagenomic
data analysis, providing a methodological basis for future research
aimed at developing machine learning models and decision-support systems
based on microbial biomarkers applied to precision agriculture.

**Keywords:** metagenomics; data science; rhizosphere microbiome; alpha
diversity; beta diversity; *Fragaria × ananassa*; bioinformatics;
hypothesis testing.

**INTRODUCTION**

Metagenomic data analysis is, in essence, a computer science problem: it
involves representing, storing, processing, and extracting patterns from
very high-dimensional data structures under scalability and
reproducibility constraints. Metagenomics ---the study of genetic
material recovered directly from environmental samples, without prior
cultivation of the microorganisms--- is one of the domains where this
computational perspective is most evident. High-throughput sequencing
technologies produce sparse count matrices on the order of thousands of
taxa per sample, whose efficient manipulation depends on specialized
data structures, sequence-classification algorithms, and workflows
designed according to software-engineering criteria: modularity,
traceability, and reproducibility (Marchesi & Ravel, 2015).

From an algorithmic standpoint, the taxonomic assignment of reads ---the
stage that transforms millions of raw sequences into a count matrix---
is solved through efficient exact *k*-mer matching against indexed
databases, such as those implemented in Kraken (Wood & Salzberg, 2014),
followed by a probabilistic refinement of abundances with Bayesian
re-estimation methods such as Bracken (Lu et al., 2017). The result is a
high-dimensional count matrix of compositional nature: the relative
abundances of each sample are constrained to the simplex (they sum to a
constant), which invalidates the naive use of the Euclidean distance and
of many classical statistical assumptions, and imposes the use of
specific transformations and dissimilarity metrics. These features
---sparsity, high dimensionality, compositionality, and volume--- place
metagenomic analysis at the intersection of data science, computational
statistics, and bioinformatics.

The rhizosphere microbiome ---the community of bacteria, archaea, fungi,
and other microorganisms that inhabit the interface between the root and
the soil--- plays a central role in plant nutrition, resistance to
phytopathogens, and crop resilience under adverse environmental
conditions (Berendsen et al., 2012; Compant et al., 2019). Numerous
studies have shown that the composition and structure of this microbial
community are closely related to the plant\'s health status, which has
driven the search for microbial biomarkers capable of anticipating or
accompanying the diagnosis of disease processes (Siegieda et al., 2024).

Strawberry (*Fragaria × ananassa*) is one of the most commercially and
nutritionally relevant fruit crops worldwide, but its production is
particularly sensitive to fungal and bacterial soil diseases, as well as
to agricultural practices and environmental conditions. In recent years,
several studies have documented consistent taxonomic differences between
healthy and unhealthy strawberry rhizospheres: genera such as
*Udaeobacter* and *Solibacter*, together with the family
Nitrosomonadaceae, tend to be more abundant in unhealthy rhizospheres,
whereas taxa such as Tepidisphaerales and *Hydrocarboniphaga* are
associated with healthy conditions (Siegieda et al., 2024). Likewise,
consortia of plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), such as
*Bacillus subtilis*, *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens*, and *Pseudomonas
monteilii*, have been reported to favorably modify the soil microbiome
and fruit quality (Nam et al., 2023), whereas phytopathogenic
microorganisms such as *Fusarium* spp., *Phytophthora* spp., or
*Botrytis cinerea* have been recurrently linked to wilting and root-rot
processes in this crop (Yang et al., 2020).

These findings raise an underlying methodological question: to what
extent do conventional statistical tools ---diversity indices and
hypothesis-based inference--- allow the differences observed at the
taxonomic level to be formally captured and validated, considering the
moderate sample size, the high dimensionality, and the compositional
nature typical of agricultural metagenomic studies? Answering this
question requires not only computing the conventional alpha and beta
diversity indices, but also evaluating their discriminant capacity
through parametric and non-parametric hypothesis tests, and exploring
the structure of the data at different hierarchical levels of the
taxonomy, from domain to species.

In this context, the present work aims to design and implement a
reproducible computational workflow ---conceived as a modular software
artifact--- for the statistical analysis of the microbial diversity of
the rhizosphere microbiome of strawberry crops, using metagenomic data
provided by the AgTech company Solena Ag. Specifically, it seeks to: (i)
establish an algorithmic procedure for quality control and preprocessing
of the taxonomic-classification data generated with Kraken; (ii)
characterize the alpha and beta diversity of the microbial communities
associated with healthy and unhealthy plants, both at the global level
and at different taxonomic levels (kingdom, phylum, family, genus, and
species); (iii) statistically contrast the observed differences through
parametric (Student\'s t-test, with equal and unequal variances) and
non-parametric (Mann--Whitney) hypothesis tests; and (iv) discuss the
potential and limitations of this workflow as a computational basis for
developing machine learning models and decision-support systems in
precision agriculture based on microbial biomarkers.

The contribution of this work is therefore both methodological and
computational: in addition to characterizing the strawberry microbiome,
it provides a reproducible, versioned, and documented pipeline ---whose
scripts are organized modularly by stage--- that can be re-executed on
new datasets or crops, and that constitutes an engineering foundation on
which to build automatic classifiers of plant health status.

Unlike studies that limit themselves to reporting descriptive abundance
differences between groups, this work emphasizes the formal statistical
validation of such differences. As will be shown below, the absence of
statistical significance at the global level does not invalidate the
existence of biologically relevant patterns at finer taxonomic levels, a
methodological distinction with direct implications for the design of
future studies and predictive models in this domain.

**METHODOLOGY**

The proposed workflow was designed as a modular computational pipeline
organized into five sequential stages: preprocessing and quality
control, computation of alpha diversity indices, computation of beta
diversity indices, taxonomic exploration at different hierarchical
levels, and statistical validation through hypothesis tests. The entire
analysis was implemented reproducibly using BASH for orchestration and
data formatting, and R/RStudio, with the Phyloseq package as the central
component, for statistical analysis and visualization.

**Computational architecture, data structures, and reproducibility**

From a software-engineering perspective, the pipeline was implemented as
a collection of numbered, independent scripts (one module per stage),
where each module explicitly loads its input, performs a well-delimited
transformation, and persists its outputs (figures and intermediate
tables). This modular decomposition favors traceability, debugging, and
selective re-execution, and allows the analysis to be treated as a
reproducible, versionable artifact rather than as a sequence of
unrepeatable interactive operations.

The central data structure is the Phyloseq object (McMurdie & Holmes,
2013), a container that coherently integrates four components: the count
matrix (taxon × sample), the taxonomy table (which encodes the hierarchy
Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species), the sample
metadata and, when available, the phylogenetic tree. The highly sparse
count matrix is stored and exchanged through the BIOM format (McDonald
et al., 2012), a binary/JSON standard optimized for large biological
observation matrices. The pipeline operations ---agglomeration by
taxonomic rank, transformation to relative abundances, and computation
of pairwise sample distance matrices--- have well-characterized
computational costs; in particular, the computation of beta diversity is
quadratic in the number of samples (on the order of O(n²·p) for n
samples and p taxa), which motivates the filtering and agglomeration
decisions adopted to keep the analysis tractable.

To ensure reproducibility, a common random seed was fixed for all
stochastic stages (for example, the NMDS ordinations), all paths were
resolved relative to the project root, and the exact execution
environment (versions of R and of the packages) was recorded. The
complete code is distributed together with this work as an R computation
directory organized by stage (00_setup, 01_preprocesamiento,
02_diversidad_alfa, 03_diversidad_beta, 04_exploracion_taxonomica, and
05_pruebas_hipotesis), accompanied by an orchestrator script and
documentation, so that all reported results and figures can be
regenerated with a single invocation.

**Study design and data source**

An observational, cross-sectional, and exploratory study was carried out
on secondary metagenomic data from the rhizosphere of strawberry crops
(*Fragaria × ananassa*), provided by the company Solena Ag, an
agricultural biotechnology (AgTech) company focused on the analysis and
improvement of the soil microbiome through molecular data and artificial
intelligence. The sequences were delivered in FASTA format, and the
results of their taxonomic classification ---previously obtained through
the Kraken classifier (Wood & Salzberg, 2014)--- were stored in JSON
files, together with the metadata of each sample, including the variable
of interest \"Treatment\", with two levels: healthy and unhealthy
(wilted). Unlike amplicon-based workflows (16S rRNA or ITS) that
typically rely on platforms such as QIIME 2 (Bolyen et al., 2019)
together with curated reference databases such as SILVA (Quast et al.,
2013), this study started from a shotgun-type taxonomic classification
generated with Kraken over the NCBI taxonomy, which conditions the
preprocessing decisions described below.

**Preprocessing and quality control**

The original dataset comprised 58 samples: 18 labeled as unhealthy and
40 as healthy. Before any diversity analysis, a quality-control process
was carried out based on a summary table provided by Solena (based on
fastp and on the Kraken output), which reported, for each sample, the
number of reads before and after quality filtering, the percentage of
bases with Phred quality above 30 before and after filtering, the number
of low-quality reads, the number of reads discarded for containing
ambiguous bases (\"N\"), the number of reads discarded for insufficient
length, the duplication percentage, the average read length (R1 and R2),
and the percentage of effectively classified reads.

A minimum threshold of 25 million reads after quality filtering was
established as an exclusion criterion. Under this criterion, five
samples were identified and removed (MP2079, MP2080, MP2088, MP2109, and
MP2137); in particular, sample MP2088 retained only two reads after
filtering, which illustrates the need for this control to avoid
unreliable diversity estimates based on insufficient sequencing depths.
After applying the filter, the dataset consisted of 53 samples: 35
healthy and 18 unhealthy, a proportion that remained constant throughout
all subsequent analysis.

The filtered data were structured as a Phyloseq object (McMurdie &
Holmes, 2013), which integrates up to four components: the abundance
table (taxon × sample count matrix), the taxonomy table, the sample
metadata and, when available, the phylogenetic tree. Beforehand, the
Kraken results in JSON format were converted to the standard BIOM format
(*Biological Observation Matrix*; McDonald et al., 2012), which allowed
efficient and interoperable storage of the abundance matrices and their
associated metadata.

**Alpha diversity indices**

Alpha diversity quantifies species richness and evenness within a sample
or community. Three complementary indices were computed on the filtered
Phyloseq object: Chao1, a non-parametric estimator of total species
richness based on the count of singletons and doubletons (Chao, 1984),
particularly robust to rare species and small samples; the Shannon index
(H\' = −Σ pᵢ ln pᵢ), which simultaneously weighs richness and evenness;
and the Simpson index (D = 1 / Σ pᵢ²), sensitive to the dominance of the
most abundant species.

These indices were computed at two moments: first, on the raw data and
on the quality-filtered data, in order to evaluate the effect of quality
control on the clarity of the biological signal; and second, on
taxonomic subsets obtained by partitioning the Phyloseq object by
kingdom (Bacteria and Eukaryota) and, within each kingdom, by
agglomerating the taxa (agglomeration function with a 10 % threshold) at
the phylum, family, genus, and species levels, with the aim of exploring
whether taxonomic resolution influences the ability to discriminate
between groups.

**Beta diversity indices**

Beta diversity evaluates how similar or different microbial communities
are between pairs of samples. Prior to its computation, absolute
abundances were transformed into per-sample relative abundances
(Phyloseq count-transformation function), in order to correct for
differences in sequencing depth between metagenomes. On these data, five
distance or dissimilarity metrics were computed: Bray-Curtis (Bray &
Curtis, 1957), Jaccard, Euclidean, Manhattan, and the Jensen-Shannon
divergence, and the results were visualized through non-metric
multidimensional scaling (NMDS). As with alpha diversity, this analysis
was replicated both at the whole-community level and in the taxonomic
subsets by kingdom, phylum, family, genus, and species.

**Multilevel taxonomic exploration**

Bar plots of absolute and relative abundance were generated, with
samples on the horizontal axis and abundance on the vertical axis,
visually differentiating between healthy and unhealthy samples. This
analysis was carried out hierarchically: first at the level of the whole
dataset (phylum), and subsequently independently for the Bacteria and
Eukaryota subsets, at the phylum, family, genus, and species levels, in
order to identify taxa whose relative abundance differed in a visually
consistent way between the two health states.

**Hypothesis tests**

To formally contrast the differences observed descriptively, null
hypotheses of equality between the healthy and unhealthy groups were
posed against alternative hypotheses of difference, using the Shannon
index as the response variable. Student\'s t-test was applied for the
comparison of two population means with small samples, under two
alternative assumptions: equal variances and unequal variances (Welch\'s
test; Welch, 1947), both at the whole-community level and restricted to
the genus *Fusarium*, given its documented relevance as a strawberry
phytopathogen. As a non-parametric complement ---and given that the
normality assumption could not always be guaranteed---, the Wilcoxon
signed-rank test in its two independent-sample version, equivalent to
the Mann--Whitney U test (Mann & Whitney, 1947; Wilcoxon, 1945), was
applied to compare the central tendency of the Shannon index
distributions between both groups. In all cases, a significance level of
α = 0.05 was used.

**Normalization, complementary analysis, and reproducibility**

In addition to the transformation to relative abundances, the
normalization of the count table with edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010) and
rarefaction were evaluated as strategies for equalizing sequencing
depth. As a complementary exploration, co-occurrence networks between
samples were generated from the Jaccard distance, to inspect whether
connectivity alone revealed a separation between health states.

The taxonomic classification of the reads was previously performed by
Solena Ag using Kraken (Wood & Salzberg, 2014), with Bayesian
re-estimation of abundances through Bracken (Lu et al., 2017). The
complete data flow followed the sequence: FASTA (raw sequences) → JSON
(Kraken taxonomic classification) → BIOM (McDonald et al., 2012) →
Phyloseq object (McMurdie & Holmes, 2013). All the preprocessing,
index-computation, and statistical-testing code was documented in
reproducible R Markdown notebooks and BASH scripts, which allows each of
the results reported in the following section to be audited and
replicated.

**RESULTS**

**Effect of quality control on alpha diversity**

Before applying the quality filter, the alpha diversity observed across
the four indices (observed richness, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) was
practically indistinguishable between the healthy and unhealthy groups,
due to the influence of a small number of samples with extremely low
sequencing depths, which visually compressed the scale of all the plots
(Figure 1A). After removing the five low-quality samples, the separation
between groups became considerably clearer (Figure 1B): the healthy
samples showed a trend toward greater diversity, particularly evident in
the Chao1 index, which, being less sensitive to the noise introduced by
low-confidence singletons and doubletons, allowed the richness
difference between groups to be appreciated more sharply. It should be
noted that, although this difference was visually perceptible, its
magnitude was not markedly large, which anticipated the inconclusive
results of the hypothesis tests presented later.

**Global beta diversity**

The five beta diversity metrics computed ---Bray-Curtis, Jaccard,
Euclidean, Manhattan, and Jensen-Shannon--- showed a consistent pattern:
the NMDS ordinations revealed a broad overlap between healthy and
unhealthy samples, with none of the metrics achieving a clear separation
between groups (Figure 2). This result suggests that, at the
whole-community level, global compositional dissimilarity is not by
itself sufficient to discriminate the plant\'s health status, and
motivated the complementary analysis in more specific taxonomic subsets
described below.

**General taxonomic composition**

At the kingdom level, the filtered dataset consisted of 8,822 OTUs
classified as Bacteria and 181 OTUs classified as Eukaryota, which
confirms the bacterial predominance characteristic of soil and
rhizosphere microbiomes. At the phylum level, considering the full set
of samples, Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria were identified as the
phyla with the highest relative abundance (Figure 3), a pattern
consistent with the literature on rhizospheres of fruit crops (Compant
et al., 2019).

**Diversity and composition by taxonomic level**

Disaggregating the analysis by kingdom and by taxonomic level made
evident patterns that were not discernible at the whole-community level.
Within the Eukaryota subset, a lower relative abundance of the phylum
Oomycota was observed in the unhealthy samples relative to the healthy
ones, as well as a specific decrease of the family Peronosporaceae in
the unhealthy samples. At finer levels (genus and species), the
taxonomic exploration identified eukaryotic taxa ---among them
*Fusarium* and *Phytophthora*--- with a higher relative presence in the
unhealthy samples, consistent with their documented role as strawberry
phytopathogens (Yang et al., 2020).

Within the Bacteria subset, the phylum-level analysis showed that
healthy samples tend to be dominated by Actinobacteria and
Proteobacteria, whereas the unhealthy samples present a distinct
internal structure, with a higher relative contribution of other
secondary phyla. Delving into the family and genus levels,
differentiating taxa were identified, among others, the family
Streptomycetaceae and the genus *Streptomyces*, as well as the family
Paenibacillaceae, the genus *Paenibacillus* and, at the species level,
*Paenibacillus polymyxa* ---a microorganism widely documented as a plant
growth-promoting rhizobacterium and biocontrol agent---, whose relative
presence showed a more defined pattern in the healthy samples (Figure
4). Consistent with previous reports in strawberry, the taxonomic
exploration also identified a higher relative presence of genera with
pathogenic potential, such as *Ralstonia*, in unhealthy samples, and of
beneficial genera, such as *Pseudomonas* and *Bacillus*, in healthy
samples (Nam et al., 2023; Siegieda et al., 2024).

Regarding diversity, both alpha and beta, computed within each taxonomic
level, the alpha diversity of Eukaryota revealed a more balanced
microbiota in the healthy plants versus a greater dominance of
potentially pathogenic taxa in the unhealthy ones, while the alpha
diversity of Bacteria showed a similar pattern, with greater diversity
and balance in the healthy samples at all evaluated taxonomic levels.
Regarding beta diversity by taxonomic level, none of the partitions
(phylum, family, genus, species) achieved a complete separation between
groups; however, the Bray-Curtis metric showed more evident
compositional differences precisely at the finer levels (genus and
species), which reinforces the value of taxonomic disaggregation over
the whole-community analysis as a strategy for identifying candidate
biomarkers.

**Hypothesis tests**

Student\'s t-test on the Shannon index at the whole-community level did
not provide sufficient statistical evidence to reject the null
hypothesis of equal means between the healthy and unhealthy groups:
assuming equal variances, the p-value was greater than 0.05, and
assuming unequal variances (Welch\'s test), the p-value was 0.1501. That
is, although the healthy samples visually showed a higher average
diversity, this difference did not reach statistical significance at the
considered level α = 0.05.

In contrast, when restricting the analysis to the genus *Fusarium*, both
versions of the t-test (equal and unequal variances) showed
statistically significant differences (p \< 0.05) between the healthy
and unhealthy groups, with the healthy group presenting a broader
distribution and a higher average diversity associated with this genus
(Figure 5). This result, restricted to a specific taxon, contrasts with
the non-significant result obtained at the whole-community level, and
illustrates that signals associated with particular taxa can be diluted
when aggregated into indices computed over the entire community.

Finally, the Wilcoxon Mann--Whitney rank test on the Shannon index at
the whole-community level yielded a statistic W = 376 and a p-value =
0.2586 (Figure 6), with a 95 % confidence interval for the difference in
central location between (−0.0124, 0.0561) and a point estimate of that
difference of 0.017 units in favor of the healthy group. Since the
confidence interval includes zero, there is insufficient statistical
evidence to reject the null hypothesis of equal central location of the
Shannon index distributions between both groups. This non-parametric
result corroborates the conclusion obtained with the t-test: with the
available sample size, it was not possible to establish a globally
statistically significant difference in Shannon diversity between
healthy and unhealthy plants, whereas the specific signal of the genus
*Fusarium* did turn out to be significant.

**DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS**

The results of this work show a pattern that is frequent in the
statistical analysis of agricultural microbiomes: the existence of
consistent biological trends and plausible differentiating taxonomic
patterns that, nevertheless, do not always reach statistical
significance when evaluated through aggregated indices at the
whole-community level. Healthy plants showed, consistently across the
different analyses, greater diversity and greater microbial balance,
both in the global alpha diversity and in that computed by taxonomic
level, whereas unhealthy plants exhibited a higher relative presence of
taxa potentially associated with disease, such as *Fusarium*,
*Phytophthora*, and *Ralstonia*, and a lower presence of taxa such as
Oomycota, Peronosporaceae, *Pseudomonas*, *Bacillus*, and
*Paenibacillus*. However, neither the global alpha diversity (Chao1,
Shannon, Simpson) nor the global beta diversity (Bray-Curtis, Jaccard,
Euclidean, Manhattan, Jensen-Shannon) allowed a statistically robust
separation between groups, and the hypothesis tests on the Shannon index
at the whole-community level ---both Student\'s t-test and the
Mann--Whitney test--- did not reach significance at the level α = 0.05.

This apparent contradiction between the descriptive trend and the lack
of global statistical significance admits several non-exclusive
explanations. First, the compositional and high-dimensional nature of
metagenomic data implies that a single index, computed over thousands of
taxa simultaneously, can dilute biological signals that are strong only
in a reduced subset of taxa ---as evidenced by the genus *Fusarium*,
whose diversity difference was indeed significant when analyzed in
isolation. Second, the sample size and imbalance (35 healthy samples
versus 18 unhealthy ones), together with the observed difference in
sequencing depth between groups, reduces the statistical power available
to detect moderate differences. Third, agricultural rhizosphere
microbiomes are subject to multiple sources of variability not
controlled in this study ---variation between crop batches, management
practices, edaphoclimatic conditions--- which can introduce additional
noise over the signal strictly associated with the plant\'s health
status.

These findings are consistent with the recent literature on strawberry
microbiomes, which reports specific taxonomic differences between
healthy and unhealthy rhizospheres ---including beneficial taxa such as
*Pseudomonas monteilii*, *Bacillus subtilis*, and *Bacillus
amyloliquefaciens* (Nam et al., 2023), and dysbiosis biomarker taxa such
as *Udaeobacter*, *Solibacter*, and Nitrosomonadaceae (Siegieda et al.,
2024)---, without this necessarily implying globally statistically
robust diversity differences. Taken together, these results reinforce
the idea that the health status of a crop is not uniformly reflected
across the entire microbial community, but in specific taxonomic
subsets, which methodologically supports the multilevel exploration
strategy adopted in this work over an analysis limited to the whole
community.

From the perspective of precision agriculture, the identification of
candidate biomarker taxa ---positive, such as *Pseudomonas*, *Bacillus*,
and *Paenibacillus polymyxa*, and negative, such as *Fusarium*,
*Phytophthora*, and *Ralstonia*--- constitutes a quantitative input of
practical value for the design of early-monitoring systems of soil
health status, as well as for the rational selection of microorganisms
with potential as bioinputs or biocontrol agents. The main contribution
of this work, however, does not lie in an isolated taxonomic finding,
but in the computational artifact developed: a reproducible, modular,
and versioned pipeline ---integrating BASH, R, the BIOM format, and
Phyloseq--- capable of systematically and traceably executing quality
control, the computation of alpha and beta diversity, multilevel
taxonomic exploration, and statistical hypothesis validation on
agricultural metagenomic data. Being organized by stages with explicit
inputs and outputs, this workflow can be reused directly on new datasets
or crops and, above all, functions as the feature-engineering layer on
which to build automatic classifiers: the abundance matrices
agglomerated by taxonomic level that the pipeline produces are precisely
the type of input representation required by supervised machine learning
models.

This study presents limitations that must be considered when
interpreting its results. The design is cross-sectional and is based on
a single crop and a single data source, which limits generalization to
other agroecological contexts. The sample size, while adequate for an
exploratory analysis, is moderate and imbalanced between groups for
high-statistical-power hypothesis tests, and no formal corrections for
multiple comparisons were applied when evaluating several taxonomic
levels simultaneously, so the patterns identified at those levels should
be interpreted as hypotheses to be confirmed rather than as conclusive
findings. Additionally, although phylogenetic diversity (for example,
through UniFrac) was considered in the theoretical framework of the
proposed workflow, a phylogenetic tree associated with the data was not
available for its effective computation in this study.

As future lines of work, we propose, first, the incorporation of
supervised machine learning models ---such as random forests--- trained
on the multilevel taxonomic abundances, in order to build classifiers of
plant health status with greater predictive capacity than the aggregated
diversity indices. This type of model is especially pertinent in
high-dimensional, moderate-sample-size scenarios such as the present
one, due to its robustness to overfitting and its provision of
variable-importance measures that would allow biomarker taxa to be
prioritized automatically; its rigorous evaluation would also require
cross-validation schemes and performance metrics (accuracy, sensitivity,
specificity, and area under the curve) that the reproducible pipeline
proposed here is naturally able to support. Second, the expansion of the
sample size and the incorporation of longitudinal designs that allow the
temporal dynamics of the rhizosphere microbiome to be observed during
disease development. Third, the integration of additional agronomic and
environmental metadata, as well as functional inference (for example,
through PICRUSt2; Douglas et al., 2020), to complement the purely
taxonomic characterization. Finally, the exploration of co-occurrence
networks and probabilistic graphical models, such as Bayesian networks,
to formally characterize the dependency relationships between key taxa
---such as *Fusarium*--- and the rest of the microbial community, a line
that was preliminarily explored in this work and that is identified as a
natural development of the proposed workflow.
